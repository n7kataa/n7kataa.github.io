Turning a camera detection into a map coordinate.

DARPA Triage Challenge · primary triage · 2025–2026

This describes the approach, not the implementation. The actual code is in a private competition repository and the imagery is covered by a data agreement, so there's no repo to link. Everything below is either public in the DARPA rules or general to the problem.

The problem.

A drone flies over a disaster scene. Its camera sees a person and a detection model draws a box around them. But a rescuer can't use pixels. They need coordinates they can walk to, and the gap between those two things is geometry, not machine learning.

Why the obvious answers fail.

The first instinct I had was to just report the drone's own GPS position. But at search altitude the camera sees a patch of ground tens of meters across, so the person could be anywhere within it. DARPA also prohibits hovering directly over casualties, so that option is closed anyway.

The second instinct got slightly closer: measure how far the detection sits from the center of the frame, scale by altitude, offset from the drone. But this assumes the camera points straight down and the aircraft is level. Neither is true. The drone tilts to move, and a few degrees of tilt moves the ground point meters away from where that formula puts it, while the answer still looks fine.

The method that works.

The fix is to stop treating the pixel as a position and start treating it as a direction. Everything after that is "bookkeeping" about what that direction means in each frame of reference.

  1. Pixel + lens calibration → a ray in the camera's frame.
  2. Rotate by how the camera is mounted → the drone's frame.
  3. Rotate by the aircraft's measured roll, pitch, and heading → world frame.
  4. Intersect with the ground plane. This is where height enters, and where direction becomes a distance.
  5. Add that offset to the drone's GPS position. Lowkey just trig at this part lol.

The decision I'd want to talk about.

Step 4 needs a height. A barometer always gives you one, but it drifts from wind, pressure changes, etc. We learned this the hard way.. A laser rangefinder is accurate to centimeters, but it drops out over vegetation and in bright sun.

The tempting design was to fall back to the barometer when the rangefinder fails, so there's always an answer. But a confidently wrong coordinate sends a rescuer to empty ground, which is worse than no coordinate at all. So the pipeline declines to answer without a fresh rangefinder reading. Knowing when not to answer is also part of the system's job.
